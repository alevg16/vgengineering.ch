01 · Consumer product · Team of five

Oyster microfibre filter

A washing-machine filter that captures clothing microfibres before they reach the ocean. From an open brief to a costed concept to a tested prototype.

1 / 4

The idea

Spin a cylindrical filter so the flow is driven outward through its whole wall. The filter area becomes the cylinder's surface, not the pipe's cross-section: far more area in the same space, and longer before it clogs.

Alejandro's part

Torque calculations, stress and fatigue FEA, the final mesh selection, prototype manufacturing and part of the concept sketching. Pump blades and the CFD study were led by teammates.

Result

On a repeat test run the printed shaft snapped exactly where the FEA had flagged. Cause: weak inter-layer shear from the print orientation. Fix: steel, a higher-modulus polymer, or an injection-moulded production part.

75.7 %fibre capture in the first test, with real microfibre-laden water
25 µmnylon mesh; beat stainless steel on mass and recyclability
12–18 Vlow-cost DC drive, sized from worst-case start-up torque

02 · Flight hardware · EuRoC 2021 · Technical lead

Darwin II airbrake

Active apogee control for a supersonic rocket: built to overshoot on purpose, then brake back to a 9 km target.

Two constraints

Symmetry. If one flap opens before another, the asymmetric force yaws the rocket at supersonic speed. Three identical flaps on one central gear-rack and one servo make that mechanically impossible.

Fail-safe. No supplier had a torsion spring of the right size, force and travel, so retraction moved to a redundant control board on its own power supply that drives the servo shut if the primary fails.

What happened

On the flight, the airbrake never deployed, and the mechanism wasn't the reason. The integrated control system was tested for the first time at the competition itself. The lesson we carry into every project: test the integrated system before it matters.

Alejandro's role

Technical lead of Endeavour, the University of Edinburgh student rocketry team (about 60 people). The airbrake was a team build.

11.3 MPapeak stress, against 250 MPa yield

03 · Test engineering · Solo project

Five test rigs for chalk

Measuring the failure strength of ordinary chalk in bending, tension, shear, torsion and compression, with no lab and no testing machine. Every rig was designed and built from workshop scrap.

1 / 6

The constraint

Each load had to be isolated cleanly, or the number would be meaningless. The first shear rig let the plate rotate and added friction to the reading; version 2 removed the parasitic moment.

The result

Consistent, physically sensible values on all five rigs, with the spread growing as the apparatus got more complex, exactly as expected. The real deliverable is the method: a defensible answer from improvised tools and clear reasoning.

Compression and tension load the same cross-section, so the almost 9× gap shows how much stronger chalk is in compression. Bending and torsion forces act through a lever arm, so those bars compare rigs, not materials.

04 · Concept & embodiment · Solo project

Seabed drilling rover

A tethered, remotely operated vehicle that drills the ocean floor: a novel machine with no off-the-shelf precedent to copy.

Architecture

A sealed chassis deployed by cable and winch, with the drill on silent-blocks like a car engine, isolating vibration and keeping the design modular.

Mobility

Four independent in-wheel motors and Mars-rover-style swing arms that adapt to uneven seabed and lock flat while drilling.

Materials

Ashby-chart selection against explicit objectives: leak-before-break for the chassis, hardness and corrosion resistance for the wheels, flexibility without failure for the arms.

Cost modelDe-risking
Bottom-up estimate by subsystem, with adaptation factors for bespoke development.
FMEADe-risking
Every subsystem rated severity × probability. The winch cable and the chassis seal came out as the risks to control first.
